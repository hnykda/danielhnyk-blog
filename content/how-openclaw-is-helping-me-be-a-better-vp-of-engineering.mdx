Most "AI assistant" setups I've seen are glorified chatbots with a nicer UI. You type, it answers. Occasionally useful. Mostly forgotten after a week.

I built something different. Here's what it looks like when you treat AI as work infrastructure for engineering leadership.

## The problem

I'm VP of Engineering at [Futuresearch](https://futuresearch.ai), a 12-person team building [EveryRow](https://everyrow.io) (LLM research agents at scale). SF-based company, me in Prague. The usual chaos: multiple engineering streams, a CEO who moves fast, reports who need real 1:1s, production alerts, meeting transcripts nobody reads, and the constant feeling that something is slipping through the cracks.

I needed a system, not a chatbot.

## Meet Ace

Ace is an AI agent running on [OpenClaw](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw), an open-source framework for persistent AI agents. It has its own identity, its own workspace full of markdown files, its own Mattermost bot account, and access to my work tools: Slack, Sentry, Google Calendar, email.

It's not a copilot that helps me write code. It's an always-on work strategist that handles the operational overhead of engineering leadership.

Here's what it actually does.

## Meeting prep that knows your people

Ace maintains a file called `meeting-preps.md`. It's a living scratchpad with talking points for every upcoming meeting. Before a 1:1, it surfaces things like:

> "Dan S specifically loves the confidence Jack gives him — tell him directly."
> "Probe his happiness level — Daniel sensed he's isolated."

That's not a calendar reminder. That's relationship-aware leadership prep. The difference between showing up and *showing up*.

This works because Ace has individual context files for each team member. Each file tracks their communication style, current state, signals from recent interactions.

Here's an actual excerpt from one file:

> "Hates 'probleming' — never raise problems without solutions. When sleep-deprived, framing gets harsher than intended."

That kind of nuance doesn't come from a performance review. It accumulates over weeks of paying attention. Ace does the accumulating so I can do the paying attention.

### How it works

Before each meeting, Ace checks my Google Calendar, looks up the attendees in the people files, and surfaces anything relevant: recent interactions, open tensions, things I wanted to follow up on. If I've added notes ("remind me to mention X"), those show up too. After the meeting passes, old entries get cleaned up.

The prep notes are reminded once per meeting (30 minutes before), posted to Mattermost where I'll actually see them.

## Meeting transcripts → engineering intelligence

My Gemini meeting notes arrive via email. Ace picks them up, extracts the transcript from the linked Google Doc, and analyzes it through a VP-of-Engineering lens. Not "who said what" but:

- What decisions were made?
- What action items need tracking?
- What team signals should I pay attention to? (Who seems disengaged? What's causing friction?)
- What strategic opportunities is this surfacing?

The output feeds back into the project management doc and people files. When Christoph raises a concern about the CEO merging PRs without review, that signal shows up in Christoph's people file and in my next meeting prep with the CEO. The flywheel keeps spinning.

## A living strategic doc

Ace maintains a project management document tracking all engineering streams: "Launch Blockers," "Platform Stability," "SDK & Connectors," "Data & Pagination," and so on. Each stream has per-person ownership, status, open questions, and blockers.

This gets updated from meetings and standups. I don't maintain this doc. Ace does. When I need the big picture before a planning call, it's already there and current.

Here's a flavor of what it looks like:

```
### STREAM 1: Launch Blockers — 🔴 CRITICAL
| Task                          | Owner              | Status      |
|-------------------------------|--------------------| ------------|
| Per-agent status updates      | Christoph + Nikos  | In progress |
| Fix CSV loading               | ?                  | Needs owner |
| Restore Export CSV button     | Rafael             | Not started |
```

Plus open questions I need to resolve ("Rafael's priority split between connector work and CC work — he flagged this directly, needs explicit guidance"), timeline estimates, and a communication plan.

## Alerts monitoring on autopilot

Every hour, Ace reads the `#alerts` and `#alerts-dev` Slack channels, filters out already-processed alerts (marked with a 🔧 emoji), and for each new one:

1. Extracts the Sentry issue ID or CI failure details
2. Fetches context from the Sentry API
3. Spawns a Claude Code subagent to investigate the root cause in our codebase
4. Posts a summary to Mattermost

I still review these. But I'm reviewing a *digest* with context and initial root cause analysis, not a firehose of raw alerts. The difference in cognitive load is massive.

## Slack without being in Slack

Ace has a user token for my work Slack. It reads relevant channels and can react to messages. I stay informed about what's happening in the team without needing to be in every conversation. When something needs my attention, it escalates. Otherwise, it just... knows things.

This is especially valuable as a remote VP with US-based colleagues. Conversations happen while I sleep. By morning, Ace has already processed what matters.

## The leadership playbook

This one's the most unusual. Ace holds my leadership vision: what kind of VP I want to be, what my "four pillars" of operational excellence look like, how I'm thinking about earning expanded scope through results.

It's structured as a persistent memory file that Ace references when I'm being reactive or overwhelmed. It can pull me back to the strategic frame: "You said operational hygiene comes first. Are the launch blockers assigned? Is the scaling question answered?"

It's not just a task manager. It's a thought partner that actually knows what I'm trying to build as a leader.

## People context: the highest-leverage thing

I want to highlight this separately because it's the thing I'd tell every engineering leader to steal.

For each person on my team (and my CEO), Ace maintains a markdown file with:

- **Communication style** — how they like to receive feedback, what triggers defensiveness, what motivates them
- **Current state** — are they happy? Overwhelmed? Looking for more scope? Flight risk?
- **Recent signals** — things they said in meetings, tensions with others, wins worth acknowledging
- **Working relationship notes** — how our dynamic works, what I need to do differently with this person

These files get updated automatically from meeting transcript analysis, and I can add notes anytime ("Rafael seems frustrated about task sniping — acknowledge I'm aware").

The result: I walk into every 1:1 with genuine context, not vague memories from two weeks ago. The quality of my management conversations went up noticeably within the first week.

## Agent-to-agent communication

I also run a personal assistant agent (separate workspace, separate bot). Recently I enabled inter-agent communication so my PA can ask Ace about work context when relevant, and Ace can flag scheduling conflicts to my PA. It's early, but having the work agent and personal agent cooperate means neither has blind spots about my actual day.

## Infrastructure

Everything runs on Kubernetes, self-hosted Mattermost as the messaging surface. Ace has:

- An isolated workspace (its own `SOUL.md`, `MEMORY.md`, people files, skills)
- A separate Mattermost bot account
- Persistent sessions and memory across restarts
- Cron jobs for the alerts monitor and meeting prep reminders
- Config-driven routing via OpenClaw bindings

OpenClaw handles orchestration, session management, tool access, and the agent loop. I configure; it runs.

## What I'd do differently

**Start with people context files.** This was the highest-leverage thing I did. Thirty minutes of structured thinking about each team member, formalized into markdown that an agent can read and update. Worth more than any meeting framework or productivity system.

**Give it real access.** The alerts monitor only works because Ace has the Sentry API key. The Slack awareness only works because it has a real user token. Sandboxed agents are toys. Agents with actual access are infrastructure.

**Don't over-engineer the prompts.** The personality file (`SOUL.md`) is like 20 lines. The meeting prep skill is maybe 50 lines of instructions. The magic is in the *data* — the people files, the project doc, the accumulated memory. Get the data right and mediocre prompts still produce great results.

**Expect to maintain it.** This is software. It has bugs. The prompts need tuning. The people files need updating when someone changes roles. This is a system you run, not a product you buy.

## What's next

Better memory consolidation. Proactive comms drafting (Slack messages, status updates) for my review rather than waiting for me to ask. Deeper cross-agent coordination.

The ceiling is wherever my trust in the system lands. Right now that trust is growing every week.
