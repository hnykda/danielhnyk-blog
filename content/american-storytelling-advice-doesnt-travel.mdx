I recently decided I wanted to get better at talking about myself. Not in a bullshit way — I wasn't trying to become a LinkedIn motivational poster. I just noticed I was chronically underselling myself. I have a genuinely interesting life and I was presenting it like a quarterly earnings report.

I suspect this is extremely common in Czech and broader European culture. We're raised on a cocktail of "don't brag," "don't stick out," and the ever-present tall poppy syndrome. The American kid who says "I'm going to be an astronaut!" gets encouragement. The Czech kid who says "Budu astronaut!" gets told "No tak, uvidíme."

So I did what any self-respecting nerd does: I went to the literature.

## The Literature Is Entirely American

And I mean _entirely_. Matthew Dicks' _Storyworthy_ — probably the best book on personal storytelling — is built around The Moth, which is essentially an American oral tradition. Olivia Fox Cabane's _The Charisma Myth_ treats charisma as a set of optimizable behaviors (very Silicon Valley). Vanessa Van Edwards runs a site literally called "Science of People" as if there's one universal science that applies to all of them.

The frameworks are consistent: craft a tight narrative, find your "5-second moment of transformation," deliver a punchy hook, land it with a clean ending. Think TED talk. Think elevator pitch. Think fast, clean, American.

I consumed all of this eagerly. I did the exercises. I found my stories. I crafted my hooks.

Then I tried saying them out loud.

## The Sound of Something Wrong

I was preparing for a dinner with friends — a group of intellectual, slightly hippie Czech people. The kind who'll spend three hours over wine discussing whether consciousness is an illusion while someone rolls a cigarette. These are my people.

I had my stories ready. Tight. Punchy. Fifteen-second setups with clean punchlines, just like Matthew Dicks taught me.

They sounded _terrible_.

Not terrible in the "I need more practice" sense. Terrible in the "this is fundamentally the wrong format" sense. Like showing up to a jazz jam session with a pre-programmed drum machine. The individual beats might be fine, but the whole thing is wrong.

When I switched to a longer, more meandering version — with digressions, with space for the other person to jump in, with texture rather than structure — it felt completely different. It felt _real_.

## A Hypothesis About Czech Storytelling

I want to be honest here: what follows is a hypothesis, not something I've researched rigorously. I haven't found academic literature on Czech conversational norms versus American ones (if you know of any, [email me](mailto:hnykda@gmail.com)). This is pattern-matching based on living in both cultures.

Czech conversational storytelling, at least in the intellectual circles I move in, seems to work fundamentally differently from the American model:

**American style** feels like a performance. There's a setup, a build, a payoff. The storyteller holds the floor. The audience's job is to listen, then react. Efficiency is valued — don't waste people's time. Get to the point. _Land the plane._

**Czech style** feels more like a shared exploration. Stories meander. There are asides. Someone interrupts with a related thought and you weave it in. The humor is dry, ironic, understated — not delivered as punchlines but embedded in the texture. The pleasure is in the journey, not the destination.

Think about the literary archetypes. America gave us Hemingway — clean, tight, every word earning its place. Czech culture gave us Švejk — a character whose entire existence is one long, cheerfully pointless digression. And Kafka, whose stories don't resolve so much as _accumulate_.

Even standup comedy seems to work differently. Czech comedians (the good ones) don't build to punchlines the same way. The humor lives in observation, in the absurdity of the situation described, in the shared recognition. It's less "ba-dum-tss" and more "...ano, přesně tak."

(Again: this is vibes, not research. I could be wrong about all of this. But my lived experience of both cultures says there's something real here.)

## The Question You Should Actually Be Asking

The self-presentation literature universally asks: _How do I tell my story effectively?_ This assumes there's one "effective" and you just need to learn the technique.

The better question is: **What does my audience actually want?**

My dinner friends don't want to be _impressed_. They don't want a tight narrative that demonstrates how interesting I am. They want to _understand_ me. They want depth, texture, the actual shape of how I think. They want to sit with an idea together, not be delivered a pre-packaged insight.

This isn't just a Czech thing, though I think it's particularly pronounced here. Any time you're preparing to talk about yourself, the first question shouldn't be about format or technique. It should be about the people you're talking to. What do they value? What makes them lean in? What makes them check out?

An American VC wants the elevator pitch. A Czech philosopher wants the ramble. A British pub companion wants the self-deprecating setup with the understated payoff. These aren't minor stylistic differences — they're fundamentally different communication contracts.

## So What Do You Do With This?

The universal advice — be present, be genuine, share your actual reactions rather than performing — probably does transfer across cultures. The stuff about _structure_ and _delivery_ likely doesn't.

Which puts me in an ironic position. I set out to learn how to talk about myself better. I consumed a library's worth of advice. I practiced the techniques. And the biggest insight I got was that the techniques don't work for my context.

I'm essentially back at square one, except now I understand _why_ I'm at square one, which is... something? There's virtually no material on non-American conversational storytelling. No Czech Matthew Dicks. No European Moth. If Erin Meyer wrote _The Culture Map_ for business communication across cultures, someone needs to write the equivalent for personal storytelling. (If that book exists and I missed it, seriously, email me.)

In the meantime, my operating framework is simple: adjust to your crowd. The best self-presentation advice might just be "pay attention to what the people around you actually respond to, and do more of that."

Which, now that I write it down, is the most Czech possible conclusion. No grand framework. No five-step system. Just: read the room, and don't be an idiot about it.

_Tak._
